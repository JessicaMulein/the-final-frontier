Production notes

Style prompt:
harp-led cinematic chamber pop, slow tempo, spacious at the open and progressively smaller, a single close intimate female alto throughout, narrator initially carrying deep cathedral reverb while the visitor's quoted lines are completely dry and close to the mic, solo harp, grand piano, low cello drone, warm strings used sparingly, no drums of any kind, domestic room tone in place of shortwave static, refrigerator hum and a clock, three unhurried knocks and a kettle as diegetic sound, one mechanical relay click at the hinge between bridge and turn, no carrier tones at all, reverb drying away to nothing across the final third, no choir, no final swell, ending quieter and smaller than it began, tender and unresolved, grieving without self-pity, acoustic and organic

Exclude styles:
fast tempo, frantic, relentless percussion, busy arrangement, dance beat, rock drums, distorted guitar, aggressive, sinister, horror, martial bombast, triumphalist fanfare, big anthemic final chorus, choir, soaring climax, shortwave static, carrier tone

Cycle note:
Standalone, and the opening of a second cycle set after the trilogy that runs The Synaptic Frontier, Faraday, The Final Frontier. It is not a fourth part of that trilogy and does not extend the war. The trilogy keeps its ending intact. This is the bill arriving for it.

The premise is a single line thrown away in The Final Frontier: "But silence has a radius." That radius had civilians in it. Two years after the last transmission, with no treaty because there was never a counterparty to sign one, a woman knocks and waits, exactly as the outro of The Final Frontier instructed. She was never intruded upon by anything foreign. She was inside the null when the defenders fired it, and what she lost was not a thought put in but a thought taken out: the language she only ever spoke with her mother, who is dead. She has not come for revenge. She has come for an accounting, which is the one thing an armistice cannot provide.

Carried motifs, deliberate and load-bearing:
- "Silence has a radius" is promoted from a passing admission in The Final Frontier to the title and the chorus. The chorus keeps the exact grammatical architecture of the trilogy's choruses, a declaration plus a widening plus a final turn, and uses it for a confession instead of a defiance. "Everyone I saved that night is somebody I did not ask" is the trilogy's own consent rule turned back on the person who wrote it.
- The knock is the inheritance. "Come in" was open invitation in The Synaptic Frontier, conditional in Faraday, and permitted after a freely given answer in The Final Frontier, whose outro set the protocol: "You may knock. Then wait." Here a stranger obeys that protocol perfectly and still cannot be helped, which is the cruelest available entrance. The song ends with the defender going out to knock on doors herself, so the motif completes by changing who has to ask.
- "Did I say yes?" is spent, and the song refuses to reuse it. Its successor is "Whose was that?", the peacetime question of provenance rather than the wartime question of authorization. It is held back to the outro and placed exactly twice, mirroring the Mindwars discipline, so it opens the next cycle instead of resolving this one. Verse 2 dramatizes it without stating it: "I'm not sure what I've still got is hers."
- "Transmit enable" from Faraday and "we had to transmit too" from The Final Frontier arrive at their final test. The visitor freely, soberly, repeatedly consents to being transmitted into. Consent was established as the rule that separates a shield from an occupier, so the visitor's yes removes the defender's principled reason for refusing transmission. It does not compel the defender; it forces her to locate the refusal somewhere other than consent. The visitor says yes, out loud, in the bridge.
- The refusal is physics, not principle, which is worse. She cannot transmit back a memory she never had. Anything she sent would arrive feeling like the recipient's own, exactly as it did for the first casualty in verse 1 of The Final Frontier. Restoration would put a stranger in her mother's chair and ask her to call it memory. The kindest available occupation is still occupation. The no is grief, not victory.
- "put this on the record" completes its third move. Private insistence in Faraday, deposition into the history of a war in The Final Frontier, and here an entry against herself.
- "Bit to the spectrum. Spectrum to bone" and "Wire to the bone" resolve into "Voice to the air, and the air finds a home." After a full trilogy of spectrum, copper, carrier tones and counterphase, the defender offers the only thing that will still arrive as hers: her spoken voice. The remedy is the oldest and slowest consensual channel there is, sound crossing a room into an ear that agreed to be there. The Synaptic Frontier promised "number to wave, and the wave finds a home," and the wave that finally finds one is a voice in a kitchen.
- Copper returns without being needed. The room is still up; she no longer lives in it.
- The kettle is deliberate domestic rhyme. It is on in the visitor's flashback at the moment she is emptied, and the defender puts one on as the only remedy she has.
- Reverb is characterization, not decoration. The defender carries the cathedral she has had since Faraday; the visitor is bone dry, a woman with no room at all. The defender's reverb dries out across the Turn. The room getting small is the sound of the armistice.
- The structural break is the point. All three trilogy songs build to a huge reverberant final chorus with choir underneath. This one has no final chorus, no choir, and no swell, and ends quieter than its own first line. An armistice that rises into a congregation is a lie. The final verse refuses the language of an anthem and replaces it with action: "So I'm going to knock. And wait."
- Shortwave static is replaced by domestic room tone, and the carrier tones are replaced by one mechanical relay click between the bridge and turn. Static was the noise floor of the spectrum; room tone is the noise floor of a life. The click is restraint made audible: consent permits transmission, but it cannot make a counterfeit memory true. The machine goes off before the kettle goes on. It answers the two tones that cancelled into silence at the end of The Final Frontier without repeating that gesture.
- Namelessness is preserved. Nothing in this world carries a proper name except the Mindwars. She is the woman in the radius. "Eleven miles from the array" supplies specificity without identity.
- "The Mindwars" is never named in this song. The war is over and the word belongs to the histories now. What gets named instead, once, spoken in the bridge and kept off the cover, is the armistice of the self: a room where no one is firing and nothing taken has come home.
