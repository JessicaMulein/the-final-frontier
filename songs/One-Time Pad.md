Cycle note:
Wartime, and the only song in the set that is not retrospective. It sits inside the period of *The Final Frontier* rather than after it, and it is not a fourth part of the trilogy or a second part of anything. The trilogy keeps its ending, *The Radius* keeps the opening of the second cycle, and *Case Zero* keeps its position beside the trilogy. Nothing here resolves the war, reopens it, expands its scale, or supplies a sender.

Two operatives share a private language built long before the tech existed, the way any two people who spend a life together build one. When mindspace becomes a channel, that language becomes the only unbreakable traffic in the war, because its key was made in a room no third party was ever in and cannot be reissued to anyone. They spend the war inside it. The song is her account, given while it is still going on.

It adds no mechanism the trilogy does not already establish: transmission exists, a channel can be person-specific, and nothing in the world can authenticate a sender. Everything the song does follows from those three facts.

The premise the other five never take up is a consensual use of the frontier. Four songs are defense and one is injury; this is the only one where somebody wanted the channel, invited it, and loved what came through. That is also why it is the most frightening one, and the note below on identity is the reason.

Carried motifs, deliberate and load-bearing:
- *Faraday* is discovered from the inside, by two people who thought doorlessness was intimacy. Faraday's bridge argues that "a room with no door isn't open. It's just gone." Here that is lived rather than reasoned: their language had no word for *come in* across nine hundred words, because they were never outside each other, and she used to offer that as the best thing about them. "A door you never built is a door nobody can shut" is the same proposition arriving as grief instead of doctrine. The song never cites Faraday's terms; a woman in a war does not have access to them.
- "Come in" takes its sixth position and changes speaker for the first time. It has been open invitation, conditional invitation, entry after a freely given answer, a protocol obeyed and still refused, and a dispatcher's "Go ahead." Here it is the one thing the code could not carry, and it is finally spoken to her, out loud, by the other person, in a room. The cycle's permission word arrives last in the only channel where it can be verified.
- The inherited security question is "Authenticate," and the song opens on it. *The Final Frontier* abandoned "Who's there?" and "friend or foe?" on the grounds that identity can authenticate a sender but cannot authorize entry. This is the song about the people for whom authentication was the entire job, and it fails in the direction the trilogy predicted from the other side: the code proves nothing about consent, and then stops proving identity too.
- "Was that you?" is the new question and is spent exactly twice, in the bridge, per the cycle's discipline. It is deliberately not the other three. "Who's there?" is identity, "Did I say yes?" is authorization, "Whose was that?" is provenance after damage, and "Which of us was sure?" is Case Zero's. "Was that you?" is authentication of one specific message, which is the only question a cryptographer actually gets to ask, and it is the one the cycle had not yet used. None of the other four phrases appears here.
- The pronoun moves to *two*, which is new. Faraday and *The Final Frontier* widen from I to we. *The Radius* refuses the widening by having no final chorus. *Case Zero* lets the swell arrive and denies it the crowd: "Don't make it a we." This one attempts the widened chorus, breaks off inside the word, and refuses "us" for a different reason than any predecessor — not because the crowd stole her experience, but because a merged us has nothing left to check against. "I don't want one mind with anybody. I want there to be two of us. Two is how you check." That reframes the cycle's consent architecture as an epistemic necessity and not only a moral preference, and it is the strongest argument the set has for the boundary it has been defending for five songs.
- The good channel and the violation are stated as the same event. Verse 2 refuses to make the telepathy beautiful: no air, no room, no delay, her arriving where the singer's own thoughts arrive, which is the exact canonical phenomenology of the first casualty's wanting. "The only difference in the world is that I asked her to." Consent is not a property of the signal. It never was. This is the cleanest statement of that in the cycle and it costs the song its own romance to make it.
- The private two-person language is deliberately placed beside *The Radius* without touching it. That song establishes a layer two people built and one death and one null destroyed, with nothing to restore from. This song shows the same kind of object in use, so the later loss has a shape a listener already understands. The two are kept distinct on purpose: the visitor's layer was taken from her, and this one was volunteered and spent. No weather words, no word for the way she said my name, no eleven miles. The rhyme is structural, not lexical.
- "Put this on the record" completes its fifth move and reverses direction. It has been a private insistence, a deposition into the history of a war, an entry against herself, and a missing line drawn by hand. Here it is entered twice, in advance, by someone who does not know how any of it comes out: "I want that on the record before the rest of it" for her own consent, and "I want that on the record too" for her partner's denial. The first four are testimony after. This is the only one filed from inside, and both entries are things that make her look worse.
- "Bit to the spectrum. Spectrum to bone" takes its sixth turn and resolves into a key: "Word to the key, and the key was a home." The chain has been a wave finding a home, a wire reaching bone, a voice crossing air, and something finding a home and leaving no mark. Here it arrives correctly signed. "Something came home in her handwriting. I still don't know whose hand" answers *Faraday*'s notebook — same handwriting, a few pages apart — and *Case Zero*'s wanting that had no handwriting at all. A forged hand is worse than no hand, because it survives inspection.
- The title is appropriated and then refused, as in *Case Zero*. A one-time pad is unbreakable only if the key is used once and destroyed, and an analyst who calls them one is being flattering rather than exact. Their key is a shared life: it cannot be used once, cannot be burned, and cannot be reissued, because reissuing it would require twelve more years. The song says the theorem is wrong in the pre-chorus and keeps the phrase on the cover anyway, so the first thing a listener meets is the misnomer the record will use for them.
- The consent protocol is not invented here, and that restraint is deliberate. It arrives as a bulletin from a committee with a number on it, and these two recognize it because they have been doing it since a bus stop. Authorship of the consent architecture belongs elsewhere in the cycle; what this song contributes is the observation that the doctrine was already somebody's manners. "We never called it a protocol" is the whole of the claim.
- The harm is real, singular, and unresolved, and she declines to look it up. A position given gladly to something that authenticated perfectly. She is never told what it was for, states plainly that not finding out is a decision rather than an innocence, and the song does not tell the listener either. Nothing about the war's scale, course, or authorship moves.
- Three accounts of the February message coexist and none is confirmed: the adversary sent it, the partner sent it and lost the sending, or the singer assembled it out of wanting to hear from her. No instrument settles it, nobody proves it, and the singer does not favor one. Her belief strength is left open on purpose so that nothing here constrains a later reading.
- No sender is depicted, quoted, characterized, or given a nature. The adversary owns the war and appears here only as a message that passes every check.
- Reverb is characterization and is inverted from *The Radius*. There the narrator carried a cathedral and the visitor was bone dry. Here the narrator has no room at all, because she has been living in a channel that has no acoustics, and the reverb is on the partner's quoted lines — a room slightly too large and too warm, the sound of something remembered rather than heard. It comes off her in the turn. The most beautiful thing in the mix is the least verifiable thing in the mix, and the arrangement says so before the lyric does.
- The second voice is a single diegetic line, not a double, a harmony, or a duet. The song is one unlayered alto throughout because the listener must be held in the singer's exact epistemic position: if you hear the partner, you have a certainty the narrator does not have. So the partner exists only as reported speech until the code is retired, and then she is audible once, dry, in the room, in plain language, saying the two things the cipher could never carry. The second voice becomes real at the moment it stops being a signal.
- Static becomes clerical. The noise floors have been the spectrum, a life, and a shift. This one is two wristwatches, a pencil, a page, and a match, because a channel with no air has no noise floor of its own and the only sounds available are the paper trail around it. The pre-final-chorus event has been a bar of silence, a null of two cancelling tones, a relay click, and an open channel with nobody on it. Here the two watches drift into exact sync and hold one bar. Nothing cancels. Two things become briefly indistinguishable, which is the thing she is most afraid of, and then they separate, which is the relief. The outro pencil writes plain words with no groups in them.
- *The Radius*'s knock appears in the lyric and deliberately not in the sound design. She travels nine hours and knocks and waits, during the war, before any bulletin taught anyone to. The three unhurried knocks stay the property of the song that owns them.
- Namelessness is kept at the surface for consistency with the other five, and is not a constraint on the work. She is a woman with a pencil and a private language. The country, the service, the assignment, the two of them, and the fourth word are held at the level of experience so nothing hardens detail that is still free to change.
- The one word they kept is never quoted, and the refusal is the form of the song rather than a coyness in it. Naming it would spend it, exactly as the war spent the other eight hundred and ninety-nine, so the lyric enacts the covenant it describes and denies the listener the single thing the listener wants. It survives because two people protected it, which is the only grace in the cycle that anybody manages to arrange in advance.
- The war is never named. She cannot name it; the histories will not have a word for it until it is safe to write one down, and the whole of her access to it is "we call it the work." That gap is inherited from *The Final Frontier*'s own account of itself and is honored rather than referenced.
