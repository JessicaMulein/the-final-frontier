I wrote the conditions before I wrote the account, because I have watched what happens to people who hand over the facts first and negotiate the terms afterwards.

The form had four boxes and the third one was the only one that mattered. *Conditions of access, in the depositor's own words. Continue on additional sheets. Conditions will be transcribed exactly and will not be edited for length.*

I used three additional sheets.

The formal dispatch review closed on the ninth. I was a party to it, so the file came to me at the training desk in an envelope with a compliments slip and no covering letter, which I have decided since was the correct amount of ceremony.

Nobody sat me down. Nobody rang. I read it at the kitchen table with my coat on and I did the arithmetic myself, because times are the one language I have never needed anybody to translate for me.

House call, incident 6839, entered 18:37:12. Adult male, sixty-two, collapse in a bathroom, unresponsive behind a blocked door, breathing not confirmed. Quarry collision, incident 6841, entered 18:37:23. One available advanced unit. Seventeen assigned to the quarry at 18:38:06.

Fire reported access to the bathroom at 18:42:09. The ordinary crew entered at 18:42:21. They confirmed cardiac arrest and began resuscitation at 18:42:44.

Those three times were on my own cards in December and I had read them a hundred times without them meaning what the review made them mean, because I had been reading them as the record of a door and the review read them as the record of a boundary. Nothing clinical could reach that patient until 18:42:09. Not Seventeen, not the crew that was already standing on the landing, not anybody, because he was behind a door that took fire four and a half minutes to open and no advanced clinician can treat a man through a door.

Seventeen was assigned at 18:38:06 and reached the quarry road at 18:44:03. Five minutes and fifty-seven seconds, on a road that ran the other way. The review had the vehicle's position at assignment and put its best case to the house at six and a half minutes. Nothing I could have sent would have arrived in time. On the review's own figures Seventeen arrives at the house no earlier than the crew that was already there and no earlier than the door.

Then the clinical section, which is the part I had not been able to guess at for four months.

The review found the arrest had occurred before the call was placed. Not at the sound the wife heard. Before it. The interval without resuscitation was already longer than the interval in which advanced intervention changes the outcome, and it had been longer before my screen ever showed me two incidents to choose between.

He was past saving before the call entered the queue. I have spent the winter and the spring establishing whether I killed him, and the answer is that the question closed before either incident reached my screen.

I sat with that for a while and waited to feel it lift.

What I want in the record is the exact size of what the review establishes, because I have now watched forty-one words about me travel round a county in a ring binder and I know what happens to a fact that is left slightly larger than it is.

The review establishes that my routing decision did not cause that death. It establishes that no available routing decision would have prevented it. It does not establish that the decision was correct, because it was not: I chose against ordinary priority on a basis I could not reconstruct then and cannot reconstruct now, and the fact that the consequence was already fixed does not convert a groundless decision into a sound one.

And it says nothing whatever about the certainty.

I read the whole file twice looking for it. There is no section in a dispatch review for where a dispatcher's judgment came from. There is a field for the decision, a field for the basis given, and a field for whether the basis is supported by the information available at the time. Mine says *departed from ordinary priority; basis not established*. That is accurate and it is the end of what any log in that building can do. The times clear the outcome. Nothing in them tells me whose the wanting was, and the two accounts I have been offered are exactly where they were in January: a woman at a bench who transmitted and cannot tell me what it reached, and something nobody can identify. I refuse both. Neither has a single piece of evidence inside my decision, and being cleared of the death does not put evidence into either of them.

So the conditions.

One. The account is sealed. It opens on my written authorization and on nothing else, except under condition two.

Two. If any published account, official summary, history, submission, or product document identifies my incident as a case of mental intrusion, the deposit opens in full, immediately, without my authorization, and without notice to whoever asks for it first.

Three. It is released whole. Not as a summary. Not as an extract. Not as a lettered illustrative case in an appendix. If the whole of it will not fit in the document somebody is writing, then it does not go in that document.

Four. The dispatch review is deposited beside it and neither is released without the other. The times do not travel without my account and my account does not travel without the times.

Five. The sentence in which I refuse both accounts of my certainty is part of the account and cannot be omitted from any release. If it is cut, the release is not authorized, whatever else was.

Six. My authorization passes to nobody. Not to family, not to an executor, not to the custodians. If I die before I use it, condition two is what remains.

The archivist read all six back to me aloud in her office. She read them slowly and she did not improve any of them. When she got to four she stopped and asked whether I wanted the two items given one reference or two, because one reference would make them harder to separate in a listing and two would make them easier to catalogue.

"One," I said.

She wrote *one*. Then she asked what title I wanted on it.

I had thought about that for a week and had nothing. The plain description is a woman routed a unit and could not say why, and that is not a title, it is the account. Anything shorter is somebody's interpretation, and the whole of what I am doing at that table is refusing to supply one.

"No title. Put the reference number and the date."

"People will call it something."

"I know. They can call it what they like. I'm not going to hand them the name as well."

She did not argue. She did not offer me a compromise, or a form of words that would work better in an index, or a reason why my second condition was unusual, and it was the first time since December that a person holding a form had accepted what I asked for at the size I asked for it.

I signed it. She sealed it in front of me and let me watch, and then she put my six sheets of conditions in a separate folder, unsealed, because conditions have to stay readable by the people who are bound by them.
