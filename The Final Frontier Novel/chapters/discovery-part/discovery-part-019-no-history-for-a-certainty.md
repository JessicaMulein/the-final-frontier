The exception notice was printed, which meant somebody had walked to the machine on the third floor for it, and she had it squared against the edge of the table when I came in.

She did not ask whether I had killed anyone. She asked for the basis of the routing departure.

Those are not the same question, and I kept them apart, because the room had already started joining them. A patient was dead. I had sent the advanced response unit elsewhere. Those two sentences fit together too easily to be left side by side unattended.

I gave her the sequence from the call cards. House collapse at 18:37:12. Quarry collision at 18:37:23. Ordinary crews assigned to both. One advanced unit available. Seventeen assigned to the quarry at 18:38:06. No measured deterioration on the road until after the assignment. Higher ordinary priority remained with the collapse.

"So why the quarry?"

"I judged the chest mechanism as greater immediate need."

"Do you still judge that?"

That was a different question again. "The road patient deteriorated and required advanced care. That doesn't establish what I knew at 18:38."

She folded her hands over the notice. "Were you overloaded?"

"No more than the room was."

"Fatigued?"

"I was six hours into shift."

"Distracted?"

"Not that I can identify."

Then she waited, the way people wait when they are giving you room to say what they have already decided you will say. "A hunch?"

"That names it without explaining it."

The procedure required her to remove me from live routing pending review. She did, and I did not object. Dev took my console, and I moved to the training desk and watched the queue from a screen that cannot assign anything.

Without a headset the room sounded incomplete. I could hear dispatchers speaking and not the callers who made their sentences necessary. Unit numbers came loose from conditions, and routes turned into answers to missing questions.

I opened a blank page and wrote only the facts available before the assignment.

*Collapse: unresponsive, breathing unknown, access blocked.*

*Collision: ambulant, chest pain after restraint, conscious, no entrapment.*

*Seventeen: one advanced unit.*

On those facts, I chose the collapse. Then I turned the page over and wrote what I had done. *Quarry.* Then I tried to build a path between the two sides of one piece of paper.

Perhaps I had heard strain in the driver's breath. The recording would answer that and the supervisor had not released it to me, and my memory supplies coherent speech and repeated denial of injury. Perhaps the quarry road added delay. It did, to the ordinary crew and to Seventeen both, and delay increases need without reversing priority. Perhaps the bathroom access problem made advanced care useless until fire arrived. Fire was already moving and reached the house before Seventeen could have.

That last one was a reason. It was not a reason I remembered considering.

A reason found afterward can explain a choice and still not be the thing that produced it. So I crossed nothing out. I marked each candidate *possible* and kept it in a separate column from *remembered*, because those words do different work and a later reader will not separate them for me.

Dev came off console on his break and stood behind the training desk. "You found anything?"

"Reasons the decision might have been defensible."

"That's something."

"It isn't the source of the decision."

He pulled the spare chair around. "Sometimes you know before you know why."

"Sometimes people say that after the outcome helps them."

"You think the outcome helped?"

The road patient had reached surgery. The collapse patient had died. One result makes the routing look necessary, the other makes it look fatal, and neither tells me what happened in me before both. "I think outcomes are trying to write the reason," I said.

He looked at the list. "You wrote quarry on the back."

"Because it was already there."

That is the closest I can come to it. The wanting did not argue with ordinary practice, overcome reluctance, interrupt a thought, or announce itself as foreign. When two calls needed one answer, the answer was already present, with the ordinary intimacy of a preference. I had not heard anything. I had not been ordered. I had wanted.

Calling it mine does not explain it. Calling it not mine would claim knowledge I do not have. I refused both comforts and wrote down that I had refused them.

At the end of shift the supervisor let me hear the first minute of each call with her present. The house line held panic, a door, the wife's breath. The road line held wind, traffic, and a man insisting he was fine. Underneath both ran the same even hiss the network puts under everything, level from the first second to the last. There was no hidden phrase in it, no cue that said *quarry*, and nothing in either voice matched the finished certainty I remembered.

I listened a second time. The result did not change.

She stopped the recordings before the unit assignment and said the formal review would preserve the rest. I wrote down the endpoint so I could not later pretend to have heard more than I had. The review was set for Tuesday at ten, and I was off live routing until then.

At home I laid the yellow strip from ticket 4716 beside the blank page. On that shift the system said east and the driver said west, and I can trace the whole correction: report, contradiction, verification, route. The paper keeps the reason.

This time the picture and the practice agreed, and I moved against them because I was sure.

I retraced the shift once in sequence and once backward from the four touches. Call tones, facts, unit status, Dev's question, assignment. The same pieces arrived at the same place both times.

Joss let himself in at nine with a bag of food from the place near his workshop and my desk fan under his arm, rewound. He had taken it three weeks ago and had been saying since that it was nearly done.

"It runs," he said. "It also makes a noise now. At this price you get one or the other."

He put the fan on the table, plugged it in, and it made the noise. Then he set a container in front of me and stood there until I picked up the fork, which he has done since we were children and which still works.

I ate. It was too hot and I ate it anyway.

He asked whether I was on nights this week. I said I was on the training desk for a while, and he said, "Right," and did not ask why, and told me instead about a customer who had brought in a food mixer with a mouse living in it. He stayed until half past ten and took the empty containers with him, because he knows I will not.

Then the flat was quiet except for the fan, and Tuesday at ten was thirty-seven hours away, and I still had nothing to bring to it but the page with two columns on it.
